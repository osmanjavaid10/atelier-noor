An hour of gold

One summer of watching a single wall in Al Quoz, and how fifty minutes of afternoon light decided the plan of a guesthouse.

By Karim Haddad

The site in Al Quoz had nothing to recommend it. A former vehicle yard, hemmed in by two warehouses, with a view of a loading bay on one side and a car park on the other. Our client wanted a guesthouse there for visiting artists. For the first month of the project we had no idea what it should look at.

Then, on a visit in late June, Sofia noticed the wall.

Fifty minutes

The neighbouring warehouse has a long blank west face, nine metres high, rendered in a pale sand colour. For most of the day it is nothing — a dull, slightly dusty plane. But at about ten to five in the afternoon the sun drops below the parapet of the building opposite, and for roughly fifty minutes it strikes that wall almost edge-on.

Every irregularity in the render throws a shadow. The wall goes from beige to gold to a deep orange and then, abruptly, to grey.

We went back the next day with a camera on a tripod and a stopwatch. And the next. Over the summer, Sofia photographed it forty-one times, at five-minute intervals, and we pinned the prints in a long strip across the studio wall in Dubai.

Designing for one hour

That wall became the reason for the building. We could not use the neighbour's warehouse, of course — but we could build our own wall, facing the same way, and make it better at catching that light.

The final courtyard wall is fluted: vertical ribs of lime render, each 40 millimetres deep, run by hand on site with a profiled template. We tested six profiles at full size before choosing one. Too shallow and the effect was faint; too deep and the shadows swallowed the colour.

Everything else in the plan arranges itself around those fifty minutes:

  • The shared table — the one place guests are encouraged to gather — sits on the east side of the courtyard, looking straight at the wall.
  • The pool between them has a black basalt floor, so in still air it doubles the wall in reflection.
  • Dinner is served at seven, but tea is served at half past four.

We spent a summer watching a wall. It was the most useful design work we did all year.

The point

Most of the time, architecture is about making good rooms for ordinary hours. But almost every site has one moment — a slant of light, a view that opens for a season, a sound — that is worth building around. Finding it usually takes patience rather than talent.

The Courtyard House opened in March. The first guests were a ceramicist from Kyoto and a printmaker from Oaxaca. By the end of their first week, the ceramicist had moved her work table outside to face the wall.
