Lime plaster and the slow wall

Why we still specify a material that takes three coats and a month to cure — and what it does to light that paint cannot.

By Inês Carvalho

The first coat goes on in the morning, thick and grey with sand, and is left rough so the next one has something to hold. The second, a week later, is finer. The third — the one you see — is little more than lime, marble dust and water, laid on in a single pass and then pressed, again and again, with a steel trowel as it stiffens. By the time it is finished, a wall has taken a month.

We still specify it on almost every project. Clients sometimes ask why, given that a good gypsum skim and two coats of paint would look similar in a photograph and cost a third as much. The honest answer is that it doesn't look similar in a room.

What lime does to light

Paint sits on a wall. Lime becomes the wall. As it cures it slowly takes carbon dioxide back from the air and turns, molecule by molecule, into limestone again. The surface that results is very slightly translucent: light enters it a fraction of a millimetre, scatters among the crystals and comes back out softened.

You notice it most at the edges of the day. At Casa do Miradouro the afternoon sun comes off the river at a low angle and lies across the stair wall for about two hours. On a painted wall it would be a bright, flat patch. On the lime it has depth — the trowel marks catch it, the tiny variations in thickness shade it, and the whole surface seems to glow from slightly inside itself.

A painted wall reflects the light it is given. A lime wall seems to keep a little of it.

Why it lasts

Lime is soft and it breathes. That sounds like a weakness, and for a while in the twentieth century it was treated as one: Lisbon's old houses were re-rendered in cement, which is hard and waterproof and, in a building made of rubble stone and timber, quietly disastrous. Moisture that once evaporated through the walls was trapped behind the cement. Timbers rotted; plaster blew.

When we opened up the walls at Miradouro, the worst damage was always behind the 1970s cement. The original lime, 130 years old, was still sound wherever it had been left alone.

So we use it partly for its beauty and partly as repair. A lime wall moves with the building, lets it dry out, and — when it is eventually damaged — can be patched invisibly with the same material, by hand, by one person in an afternoon.

What we ask of our builders

We work with three plastering crews in Portugal and one in the UAE who still do this well. We ask for the same things every time:

  • Sample panels on site, at least a square metre, in the room where the plaster will go — never in a workshop.
  • Pigment from earth, not from tins: ochres and umbers, mixed in the bucket, in quantities written down so the next batch matches.
  • No sealer, no paint. If a wall marks, it can be wiped, and in time the marks become part of it.

It is slow. Our programmes allow for it from the first week. But a month spent on a wall is a small fraction of the hundred years we expect it to stand.
