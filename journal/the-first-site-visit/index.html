The first site visit

What we look for, what we measure and what we leave alone when we walk into a building for the first time.

By Rui Mendonça

Every project begins the same way: one of us walks into the building, or onto the land, and spends a day there doing very little.

It is the most important day of any commission, and the easiest to rush. Clients are keen to see drawings. We are keen to start them. But almost every mistake we have made over fourteen years can be traced back to something we didn't notice on the first visit.

What we bring

Not much. A laser measure and a long tape. A compass — a real one, because phones disagree with each other indoors. A notebook with squared paper. A camera, but used sparingly. And time: we book the whole day, from before sunrise if we can.

What we look for

Where the light falls, and when. We note the sun at arrival, at noon and in the late afternoon, and mark it on a rough plan. In Lisbon we pay attention to the river light, which bounces up from the south and fills rooms in a way no sun-path diagram predicts. In Dubai we look for shade first.

What the building is made of. We tap walls, lift a corner of carpet, look into cupboards and under stairs. The fabric of a building tells you what it can bear and what it will tolerate. Lime or cement? Timber joists or concrete? Original or repaired?

What is good and should stay. This is the hardest thing to see, because good things are usually unremarkable: a well-proportioned window, a floor worn into a path, a stair with an easy rise. We make a list and treat it as a promise.

Sound and smell. Traffic, neighbours, a school playground, damp. None of it shows in a photograph.

What we measure

Everything we will later regret not measuring. Ceiling heights in every room, because old buildings are never level. Wall thicknesses at every opening. The fall of the street. The height of the neighbour's windows. At Pátio das Janelas we measured the width of every iron shutter, all 112 of them; three years later, every one was re-hung exactly where it had been.

What we leave alone

Opinions. On the first visit we try not to design anything — not even in conversation. The temptation is enormous, especially with an enthusiastic client in the room, but an idea spoken aloud on day one has a way of surviving long after it should have been abandoned.

The first visit is for listening — to the client, and to the building.

What comes out of it

A measured survey, drawn up in the studio the following week. A short written note about the light. A list of things that must stay. And usually one surprise: the thing nobody mentioned, that turns out to matter most. At Miradouro it was the view from the cellar. At the Courtyard House it was a wall next door.

Neither appeared in the brief. Both shaped the building.
